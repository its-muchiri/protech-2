# Electrical Installation & Wiring in Kenya: Complete Guide 2026

## Introduction

Electrical installations form the nervous system of every building in Kenya—from modest homes in Nairobi's suburbs to sprawling commercial complexes and industrial plants. A well-designed, professionally installed electrical system delivers safety, reliability, and efficiency, while a poorly executed one poses fire risks, shock hazards, and constant maintenance headaches.

This comprehensive guide covers electrical installation and wiring for domestic and commercial properties in Kenya. It explores regulatory requirements, best practices, key components, costs, and the growing importance of smart and energy-efficient electrical solutions. Whether you are building a new property, renovating, or troubleshooting an existing system, this guide provides the knowledge you need to make informed decisions.

## Regulatory Framework in Kenya

### The Energy Act and Licensing

**EPRA (Energy and Petroleum Regulatory Authority)** is the primary regulator for electrical works in Kenya. Under the Energy Act, electrical installation work must be carried out by certified electricians registered with EPRA.

**Electrical Worker Categories** (EPRA certification):
- **Electrical Assistant**: Works under supervision
- **Electrical Technician**: Qualified for standard installations
- **Electrical Engineer**: For complex and high-voltage installations

**Key Requirements**:
- Electrical wiring and installation work must be done by EPRA-certified professionals
- Work must comply with the Kenyan Electrical Wiring Code (previously the Electricity Wiring Regulations)
- Certification numbers are verifiable through EPRA's online system

**Consequences of Uncertified Work**:
- Invalid insurance claims after fire/electrical incidents
- Potential criminal liability for serious incidents
- Kenya Power may refuse to connect a property with non-compliant installations
- Fines and legal action for practicing without certification

### The Wiring Regulations

Kenya's electrical wiring code prescribes:
- Cable types and sizes for different applications
- Installation methods (conduit, trunking, cable trays)
- Protection requirements (fuses, circuit breakers, RCDs)
- Earthing and bonding standards
- Minimum clearance and access requirements
- Socket, switch, and fitting standards

**Compliance Check**: When hiring an electrician, verify EPRA certification. Request to see the certificate. For new builds, county approval and Kenya Power connection require evidence of compliant installation.

## Key Components of an Electrical Installation

### Consumer Unit / Distribution Board

**The "fuse box"**: The distribution board (DB) is where incoming power is split into circuits for different areas and functions of the building.

**Components**:
- **Main switch**: Isolates the entire building
- **MCBs (Miniature Circuit Breakers)**: Individual circuit protection
- **RCD (Residual Current Device) / RCCB**: Detects earth leakage and cuts power to prevent shock
- **RCBO**: Combines MCB and RCD functions per circuit
- **Neutral and earth bars**: For circuit connections
- **Main earth connection**: To the earth electrode

**Modern DB vs. Old Fuse Box**:
- Modern MCB/RCD protection is far safer than traditional rewireable fuses
- RCDs provide life-saving protection against electric shock
- Circuit labeling improves maintenance and troubleshooting
- Upgrading an old fuse box is one of the highest-value safety improvements

### Circuit Types

**Lighting Circuits**: Power for light fittings; typically 6A protection
**Socket Circuits**: Power outlets; typically 16A or 32A (ring vs. radial)
**Cooker Circuits**: Dedicated high-power circuit for cookers; typically 32A
**Water Heater Circuits**: Dedicated circuits for instantaneous/electric water heaters; typically 16-32A
**Submain Cables**: Feed the DB or power sub-distribution panels
**Dedicated Appliance Circuits**: For air conditioners, washing machines, ovens, etc.

### Cables and Conductors

**Cable Types**:
- **PVC-insulated singles**: Installed in conduits (common in domestic wiring)
- **Twin and earth (T&E)**: For domestic ring/radial circuits
- **SWA (Steel Wire Armoured)**: For underground feeds and outdoor runs
- **Mi cable**: Fire-resistant for critical circuits
- **Fire-resistant cables (FP200)**: For emergency systems

**Cable Sizing**: Correct sizing prevents overheating and voltage drop. Standard domestic cable sizes: 1.5mm² (lighting), 2.5mm² (sockets), 4mm², 6mm², 10mm², 16mm² (high loads, sub-mains).

**Copper vs. Aluminium**:
- Copper: Better conductor, preferred for installations
- Aluminium: Cheaper, used for larger sub-mains and utility service lines
- **Important**: Mixed copper/aluminium connections require special connectors to prevent corrosion

### Earthing and Bonding

**Earth Electrode System**: 
- Earth rod(s) driven into ground
- Correctly sized earth conductor to the consumer unit
- Required for all building installations

**Purpose**:
- Protect against electric shock (fault current path)
- Protect equipment
- Ensure RCDs and breakers operate correctly on fault

**Bonding**:
- Equipotential bonding of metal pipework, structural steel, and metal services
- Prevents dangerous potential differences between exposed metal parts

**Testing**: Earth loop impedance and continuity testing are part of proper installation verification.

### Protection Devices

**MCB (Miniature Circuit Breaker)**:
- Protects circuits from overload and short circuit
- Trip curves: Type B (domestic), Type C (commercial/industrial)
- Not replaceable trip unit—replace whole device on failure

**RCD (Residual Current Device)**:
- Detects earth leakage (fault current to earth)
- Typical sensitivity: 30mA (personal protection), 100mA/300mA (fire protection)
- Trip time: typically within 40ms
- Essential protection in wet areas (bathrooms, kitchens, outdoor circuits)

**Surge Protection Device (SPD)**:
- Protects against voltage surges (lightning, switching surges)
- Increasingly important for sensitive electronics (computers, TVs, security systems)
- Install at the main panel

## Domestic Electrical Installation

### Scope of Domestic Work

- Full rewiring of existing homes
- New build electrical installation
- Additional circuits and socket upgrades
- Lighting design and installation
- Appliance circuit installation
- Electrical fault diagnosis and repair
- Safety inspections and certification

### The Installation Process

1. **Assessment**: Site visit, requirements gathering, load assessment
2. **Design**: Circuit layout, cable sizing, protection coordination
3. **Approvals**: As required (renovations may need county permits)
4. **Installation**: First fix (conduits/cables), second fix (accessories)
5. **Testing**: Insulation resistance, earth loop, RCD function
6. **Certification**: Electrical certificate of compliance issued

### Wiring a New Home: What to Expect

**First Fix** (before plastering):
- Conduit installation
- Cable pulling
- Back boxes for switches and sockets
- DB location and wiring

**Second Fix** (after plastering/painting):
- Fitting switches, sockets, light fittings
- Connecting accessories
- Testing and commissioning

**Typical Domestic Installation Cost (2026)**:
- New 3-bedroom house full wiring: KES 120,000 - 250,000 (materials + labor)
- Materials: KES 80,000 - 150,000
- Labor: KES 40,000 - 100,000
- Rewiring an existing house: KES 150,000 - 350,000

*Costs vary significantly with house size, specification quality, and contractor.*

### Electrical Safety for Homes

**Checklist for homeowners**:
- RCD protection present on all circuits (modern requirement)
- No damaged cables, exposed wires, or flickering lights
- Sockets not overloaded (avoid multiplug adapters on high-load circuits)
- Older wiring inspected by professional (homes over 25 years old)
- Smoke alarms fitted and tested
- No DIY electrical work—always use certified electricians
- Regular inspections (every 5 years or on significant change)

**Warning signs of electrical problems**:
- Frequent tripping of breakers
- Burning smell from sockets/DB
- Warm or discolored sockets/plugs
- Flickering or dimming lights
- Buzzing sounds
- Electric shocks from appliances

**Three-Phase Supply**: Many commercial premises require three-phase power (415V) for equipment and machinery.

**Higher Loads**: Larger DBs, submain distribution, multiple circuits.

**Specific Systems**:

- Data and communication cabling
- HVAC electrical supplies

**Regulatory Requirements**:

## Power Audits

### What is a Power Audit?

A systematic assessment of a building's electrical systems to identify:
- Energy inefficiencies and waste
- Overloaded circuits
- Aging or unsafe components
- Opportunities for energy savings
- Capacity for future expansion

### Why Conduct a Power Audit?

**Energy Cost Reduction**:
- Identify energy-hungry equipment
- Recommend lighting upgrades (LED conversion)
- Identify operational inefficiencies
- Typically 10-30% potential savings identified

**Safety**:
- Identify fire risks and hazardous conditions
- Verify protection devices function correctly
- Confirm compliance with regulations

**Reliability**:
- Identify overloaded circuits and components before failure
- Plan upgrades proactively

**Power Quality**:
- Detect voltage fluctuations, harmonics, power factor issues
- Verify supply stability for sensitive equipment

### Audit Process

1. **Data collection**: Bills, equipment inventory, usage patterns
2. **On-site survey**: Panel inspections, load measurements, thermal imaging
3. **Analysis**: Identify issues, quantify savings
4. **Report**: Findings, prioritized recommendations, cost-benefit analysis
5. **Implementation**: Professional remediation of identified issues

### Power Audit Cost in Kenya

- Residential: KES 5,000 - 20,000
- Small commercial: KES 15,000 - 50,000
- Large commercial/industrial: KES 50,000 - 250,000+

*Cost depends on property size, complexity, and report depth. Energy-saving recommendations often pay for the audit quickly.*

## Solar and Generator Integration

### Solar Electrical Integration

Solar installations require professional electrical integration:
- Inverter connection to the DB
- Changeover between grid and solar
- Battery system wiring
- Net metering arrangements (where applicable)
- Proper protection and earthing

**Grid-Tie vs. Hybrid**:
- **Grid-tie**: Solar feeds grid, no battery (export or net metering)
- **Hybrid**: Battery storage; solar/battery/grid mix

**Compliance**: EPRA requires certified solar installers and compliant installations. See our Solar Power Guide for details.

### Generator Integration

- Generator feed via ATS or manual changeover
- Proper earthing of generator installation
- Correct cable sizing for generator output
- Interlocking to prevent simultaneous grid/generator supply
- Load management to avoid overloading the generator

**Critical**: A licensed electrician must handle generator-to-panel connections to prevent backfeeding risks and ensure safety.

### Whole-Property Power Management

Modern installations can combine solar, battery, generator, and grid:
- **Smart switching**: Priority logic (solar → battery → grid → generator)
- **Load shedding**: Non-essential loads dropped during outages
- **Monitoring**: Real-time usage tracking via apps
- **Backup prioritization**: Critical circuits (fridge, lights, security) always powered

This integrated approach is increasingly common in Kenyan homes and businesses seeking resilience and cost control.

### Do I need EPRA certification for my electrical work?

**The electrician must be EPRA-certified.** As a property owner, you should:
- Verify the electrician's EPRA certification before work begins
- Request the certificate of compliance after installation
- Keep records for insurance and future reference

**DIY electrical work** is not recommended and may invalidate insurance. Only certified professionals should perform electrical installation and major repairs.

### What is an RCD and why do I need one?

**RCD (Residual Current Device)** monitors the current flowing in live and neutral conductors. If the difference exceeds its threshold (e.g., 30mA), it trips within milliseconds—interrupting the circuit before a shock becomes lethal.

**Why you need it**:
- Life-saving protection against electric shock
- Required for modern compliant installations
- Protects against electrical fire risk
- Detects earth faults that MCBs alone cannot

**Note**: A system with only MCBs (older installations) provides overload protection but NOT earth-leakage shock protection. Upgrading to RCD protection is strongly recommended.

### Why does my circuit breaker keep tripping?

**Common causes**:
- **Overload**: Too many appliances on one circuit
- **Short circuit**: Faulty wiring or appliance
- **Earth fault**: Insulation breakdown, water ingress
- **Faulty appliance**: Defective device drawing excessive current
- **Incorrect rating**: Breaker too small for circuit load

**Action**: If a breaker trips repeatedly, do NOT keep resetting it. Unplug devices, identify the cause, and have a qualified electrician diagnose the issue. Persistent tripping indicates a genuine fault.

### How often should I have my electrical installation inspected?

**Recommendations**:
- **New installation**: Full test and certificate on completion
- **Routine**: Every 5 years for homes; every 3-5 years for commercial properties
- **After incidents**: Following any significant electrical event (burning smell, arcing, water damage)
- **Before purchase**: When buying an older property
- **On renovation**: Before extending or modifying a circuit

**Also** inspect when: Adding high-power appliances, experiencing frequent breaker trips, or noticing signs of aging wiring.

### Can I use extension cables permanently?

**Discouraged**:
- Extension leads are designed for temporary use
- Permanently loaded extensions overheat, especially coiled ones
- Daisy-chaining multiplugs is a common fire cause

**Better solutions**:
- Install additional sockets
- Add circuits for permanent high-load appliances
- Use properly rated power strips with surge protection for temporary setups

**Warning signs**: Warm plugs, scorched sockets, melted adapters—all indicate overload.

### What's the difference between MCB and RCD?

**MCB (Miniature Circuit Breaker)**:
- Protects the circuit from overload and short-circuit current
- Doesn't detect earth leakage
- Designed to protect the wiring and prevent overheating/fire

**RCD (Residual Current Device)**:
- Detects earth leakage current (including through a person)
- Trips on small fault currents (30mA) to prevent shock
- Designed to protect people

**Best practice**: Use both. RCBOs combine both functions per circuit. Modern installations include RCD protection (or RCBOs) plus MCBs.

### Do I need a permit for electrical work in Kenya?

**For new installations**: Yes—building and electrical works typically require county approval. Kenya Power also requires a compliance certificate (from a certified electrician) before connecting a new supply or upgrade.

**For renovations**: Permits may be required depending on scope and county regulations. Consult your county government and certified electrician.

**Penalties**: Unapproved installations can face inspection failures, disconnection, or fines.

### How do I find a reliable electrician in Kenya?

1. **Verify EPRA certification** (check the EPRA website or ask to see the certificate)
2. **Seek recommendations** from trusted contacts
3. **Check recent work** and ask for references
4. **Compare detailed quotes** from 2-3 contractors
5. **Use contracts** with clear scope, timeline, and payment terms
6. **Confirm warranties** in writing

**Pro-Tech tip**: A slightly higher quote from a certified professional with a documented track record is almost always worth it compared to cheaper uncertified work.
