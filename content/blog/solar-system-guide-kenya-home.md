# Solar Power for Homes in Kenya: Complete Guide 2026

## Introduction

Kenya enjoys some of the highest solar irradiance levels in the world, with an average of 4-6 kWh/m²/day across most regions. This abundant solar resource, combined with rising electricity costs and EPRA (Energy Regulatory Authority) incentives, has made solar power increasingly attractive for Kenyan homeowners. However, navigating the solar market in Kenya requires understanding local conditions, regulatory requirements, and the practical considerations of implementing a solar solution tailored to Kenyan households.

This comprehensive guide explores solar power options for Kenyan homes, providing practical insights for homeowners considering a transition to solar energy. From understanding your energy needs to selecting the right system type, navigating regulations, and ongoing maintenance, this article aims to equip you with the knowledge to make an informed decision about solar power for your home.

## Kenya's Solar Potential and Energy Context

### Solar Irradiance Across Kenya

Kenya's position near the equator grants it consistent year-round sunlight. However, solar potential varies by region:

- **Northern Kenya** (Marsabit, Mandera, North Eastern): Highest irradiance, 5-6+ kWh/m²/day
- **Rift Valley** (Nakuru, Naivasha, Eldoret): Excellent potential, 4.5-5.5 kWh/m²/day  
- **Highland Regions** (Nairobi, Kiambu, central Kenya): Good potential, 4-5 kWh/m²/day, but slightly lower due to cloud cover
- **Coastal Regions** (Mombasa, Kilifi, Kwale): Good potential, 4.5-5 kWh/m²/day, with humidity considerations

This consistent solar resource means that well-designed solar systems can deliver substantial energy savings across Kenya, though system design should account for local conditions.

### Rising Electricity Costs

Kenya Power and Lighting Company (KPLC) tariffs have been steadily increasing, with domestic consumers particularly affected by rising per-kWh rates and fixed monthly charges. For many households, electricity bills now represent a significant portion of monthly expenses, making solar an increasingly attractive alternative for long-term cost mitigation.

### EPRA and Regulatory Framework

The Energy Regulatory Authority (EPRA) oversees Kenya's solar industry, ensuring quality standards and consumer protection. Key regulatory aspects include:

- **EPRA certification** is required for solar installers and system designers
- **Net metering** policies allow excess solar energy to be fed back to the grid (though terms evolve)
- **Import duties** and taxes affect solar equipment pricing
- **Building codes** increasingly incorporate solar readiness requirements for new constructions

Understanding these regulations is essential for compliant, effective solar installations.

## Determining Your Solar Needs

### Energy Consumption Analysis

The first step in solar planning is understanding your current energy usage:

**Review KPLC Bills**: Examine the past 12 months of electricity bills to determine:
- Average monthly consumption in kWh
- Peak usage patterns (times of day when consumption is highest)
- Seasonal variations (typically higher during dry seasons for pumping, or rainy seasons for lighting)

**Typical Kenyan Household Consumption**: 
- Small household (1-2 people): 100-200 kWh/month
- Medium household (3-4 people): 200-400 kWh/month  
- Large household (5+ people) or with appliances: 400-800+ kWh/month
- Households with electric cooking or extensive air conditioning: 800+ kWh/month

**Beyond KPLC Bills**: Consider future additions that may increase consumption:
- Electric vehicle charging (if applicable)
- Expanded household size
- New appliances (air conditioners, electric water heaters)
- Home-based business operations

### Peak Load Assessment

Understanding your peak power demand (kW) is crucial for system sizing:

- **Identify high-wattage appliances**: Electric kettles (1,000-3,000W), refrigerators (100-250W running, 800-1,200W starting), televisions (100-400W), lighting (LED: 5-20W per bulb)
- **Simultaneous usage**: Note which appliances often run together (e.g., lights, television, and refrigerator in evening)
- **Motor-driven appliances**: Pumps, fans, and compressors have higher starting currents than running currents

A comprehensive load assessment typically reveals that peak demand may be 1.5-2x the average consumption pattern, influencing inverter and battery sizing decisions.

## Solar System Types for Kenyan Homes

### Off-Grid Solar Systems

Off-grid systems operate independently of the KPLC grid, requiring battery storage for nighttime and cloudy-day usage. These are ideal for:

- **Remote locations** with unreliable or no grid access
- **Households seeking complete energy independence**
- **Areas with frequent extended outages**

**Components**:
- Solar panels (generating capacity based on daily needs)
- Charge controller (regulates panel output to batteries)
- Battery bank (typically lithium-ion or tubular lead-acid for Kenyan conditions)
- Inverter (converts DC to AC for household appliances)
- Backup generator (optional, for extended cloudy periods)

**Typical Sizing for Kenyan Homes**:
- Basic system (lights, phone charging, TV): 1-2 kW panel, 4-8 kWh battery
- Standard system (adds refrigerator, fans): 3-5 kW panel, 8-16 kWh battery
- Comprehensive system (adds cooking, AC support): 5-8 kW panel, 16-40 kWh battery

**Cost Range**: KES 150,000 - KES 1,500,000+ depending on capacity and component quality

### Grid-Tie Solar Systems

Grid-tie systems connect to the KPLC grid without battery storage, feeding excess solar power back to the grid (where net metering is available) and drawing from the grid when solar production is insufficient. These are suitable for:

- **Grid-connected homes** seeking to reduce electricity bills
- **Areas with reliable grid** supplementation needed
- **Households with net metering availability** (increasingly available in Nairobi and expanding regions)

**Components**:
- Solar panels
- Grid-tie inverter (no battery bank required)
- Electrical connection to main distribution board
- Bi-directional meter (for net metering)

**Advantages**:
- Lower upfront cost (no batteries)
- Simpler maintenance
- Potential revenue from excess export (where available)
- Seamless grid integration

**Limitations**:
- No power during grid outages (unless with special hybrid inverters)
- Dependence on grid availability
- Net metering policies and rates vary by region and are subject to change

**Typical Sizing**: 3-7 kW systems common for residential applications, offsetting 50-100% of consumption

**Cost Range**: KES 100,000 - KES 800,000+ depending on system size

### Hybrid Solar Systems

Hybrid systems combine grid-tie functionality with battery backup, offering the best of both worlds. These are increasingly popular in Kenya and are ideal for:

- **Households experiencing frequent short outages** but with grid access
- **Those wanting battery backup** without full off-grid independence
- **Applications requiring specific backup duration** (e.g., 4-8 hours for essential loads)

**Components**:
- Solar panels
- Hybrid inverter (manages grid, batteries, and loads)
- Battery bank (smaller than full off-grid, typically 5-10 kWh for home essentials)
- Grid connection

**Advantages**:
- Power during grid outages (for backed-up circuits)
- Reduced battery costs vs. full off-grid
- Flexibility to use grid or batteries as needed
- Potential for future expansion to full off-grid

**Considerations**:
- Higher initial cost than grid-tie alone
- More complex system management
- Battery replacement cycle every 5-15 years depending on type

**Typical Sizing**: 3-7 kW panels with 5-15 kWh battery backup

**Cost Range**: KES 200,000 - KES 1,200,000+ depending on capacity and component quality

### Solar Water Heaters

Solar water heating is a distinct technology from photovoltaic (electricity-generating) solar, focusing specifically on heating water using solar thermal energy. These are highly suitable for Kenyan households and offer:

- **Significant gas/electricity savings** for water heating (typically 60-80% of water heating energy)
- **Quick payback period** (typically 2-4 years)
- **Simple technology** with minimal maintenance
- **Year-round functionality** even on cloudy days (though reduced efficiency)

**Types**:
- **Thermosiphon systems**: Natural circulation, roof-mounted storage tank, typically 150-300 liter capacities
- **Pump-circulated systems**: Pump-driven, separate storage tank, more flexible installation options
- **Flat plate collectors**: Most common, durable, effective in Kenya's climate
- **Evacuated tube collectors**: Higher efficiency, particularly useful in cooler regions or for higher temperature requirements

**Typical Domestic Systems**: 150-300 liter systems for 4-6 person households, installed on rooftops facing north (in Southern Hemisphere) or with optimal sun exposure

**Cost Range**: KES 30,000 - KES 150,000 including installation, depending on capacity and type

**Considerations for Kenya**: 
- Roof structure and orientation
- Existing water heating method (electric, gas, wood)
- Household hot water usage patterns
- Local climate (effective even in cooler highland regions)

## Solar Component Considerations for Kenya

### Solar Panels (Photovoltaic Modules)

**Panel Types Available in Kenya**:
- **Monocrystalline**: Higher efficiency (17-23%), better performance in limited space, higher cost
- **Polycrystalline**: Moderate efficiency (15-17), lower cost, slightly larger area required for same output
- **Thin-film**: Lower efficiency, flexible applications, niche uses

**Kenyan Market Realities**:
- Most panels installed are Chinese-manufactured (approx. 70-80% of market), with increasing presence of Indian, Turkish, and local options
- Key brands in the market: JA Solar, Canadian Solar, Trina Solar, Longyi, and various local assemblers
- Panel efficiencies typically range from 17-23% for monocrystalline, 15-17% for polycrystalline
- Warranties typically 10-12 years product, 25 years performance (guaranteed minimum output percentage after 25 years)

**Temperature Considerations**: Solar panel efficiency decreases slightly with increasing temperature (typically -0.3% to -0.5% per °C above 25°C). While Kenya's ambient temperatures can be high, the abundant sunlight more than compensates, and quality panels are designed to operate effectively in tropical conditions.

### Inverters

**Inverter Types**:
- **String inverters**: Single inverter for entire array, cost-effective for uniform roof orientations
- **Micro-inverters**: One inverter per panel, higher cost but individual panel optimization, beneficial for shading scenarios
- **Hybrid inverters**: Manage solar, batteries, and grid interaction, essential for hybrid systems

**Sizing Considerations**: Inverter capacity should match or slightly exceed peak solar array output. For a 5 kW solar system, a 5-6 kW inverter is typical.

**Kenyan Market**: Quality varies significantly; reputable brands include SMA, SolarEdge, Huawei, and local/international alternatives. MPPT (Maximum Power Point Tracking) functionality is essential for optimizing performance under varying conditions.

### Batteries (for Off-Grid and Hybrid Systems)

**Battery Technologies Available**:
- **Tubular Lead-Acid**: Traditional, cost-effective, 3-5 year lifespan, requires maintenance (water topping, equalization charging)
- **Lithium-Ion (LiFePO4)**: Higher cost, 8-15 year lifespan, maintenance-free, deeper discharge capability, lighter weight
- **Gel Batteries**: Sealed, maintenance-free, moderate lifespan, sensitive to charging parameters

**Kenyan Market Considerations**:
- Lead-acid batteries remain dominant due to lower upfront cost
- Lithium-ion adoption growing as costs decrease and awareness increases
- Imported batteries vs. local assemblers (some local companies assemble battery systems using imported cells)
- Depth of discharge (DoD) considerations: Li-ion typically 80-90% DoD vs. 50% for lead-acid, affecting usable capacity

**Sizing Guidance**: For typical Kenyan home, 8-16 kWh of battery capacity provides 1-2 days of essential load backup. Critical load selection (refrigerator, lighting, phone charging, limited electronics) extends backup time.

### Mounting Structures

**Roof-Mounted Systems**: Most common for residential Kenya, utilizing roof space efficiently. Considerations:
- Roof type and age (corrugated metal, tile, concrete)
- Structural capacity (most residential roofs adequately support solar)
- Orientation and tilt (optimal: north-facing at angle equal to latitude ≈ 1°S for Kenya)
- Roof age and expected remaining life (install solar on roofs with remaining life exceeding system life)

**Ground-Mounted Systems**: Suitable for properties with available land, allowing optimal orientation and easier maintenance access.

**Tracking Systems**: Single-axis or dual-axis trackers follow the sun, increasing energy capture by 15-30%, but add cost and complexity. Generally not cost-effective for typical residential Kenyan installations.

## Financial Considerations

### Upfront Costs

Solar system costs in Kenya vary significantly based on technology, capacity, and quality:

**Off-Grid Systems**:
- Basic (1-2 kW, essential loads): KES 150,000 - KES 400,000
- Standard (3-5 kW, whole home without AC): KES 400,000 - KES 800,000
- Comprehensive (5-8 kW, including AC support): KES 800,000 - KES 1,500,000+

**Grid-Tie Systems**:
- Small (3 kW, ~30% offset): KES 100,000 - KES 250,000
- Medium (5 kW, ~50% offset): KES 200,000 - KES 400,000
- Large (7 kW, ~80-100% offset): KES 350,000 - KES 700,000+

**Hybrid Systems**: Typically 20-30% higher than comparable grid-tie due to battery addition

### Financing Options

Several financing mechanisms make solar more accessible:

**Supplier Payment Plans**: Many solar companies offer 6-24 month installment plans, sometimes with zero interest promotional periods.

**Bank Financing**: 
- Personal loans from commercial banks (KCB, Equity, Co-operative, etc.)
- Term loans typically 2-5 years
- Some banks offer green loans or specific solar financing products
- Interest rates vary (currently approx. 13-18% p.a. for personal loans)

**Hire Purchase**: Structured payment plans where ownership transfers upon final payment, often through solar suppliers or partnered financial institutions.

**Pay-As-You-Go (PAYG)**: Mobile money-based systems where users pay for energy service incrementally, popular in off-grid and semi-off-grid contexts. Companies like M-KOPA, Azuri, and Bboxx operate in this space, typically for smaller systems.

**Government Incentives**: 
- EPRA regulation framework ensuring quality standards
- Potential tax incentives (though specific measures evolve; consult current tax regulations)
- County government initiatives sometimes offer support or streamlined permitting

### Return on Investment Analysis

**Typical Payback Periods**:
- **Grid-tie systems**: 3-7 years depending on electricity consumption, system cost, and offset percentage
- **Off-grid systems**: 5-8 years, primarily driven by avoided generator fuel costs and grid extension avoidance
- **Solar water heaters**: 2-4 years, typically shortest payback of solar technologies

**Key ROI Factors**:
- Current and projected electricity tariffs (KPLC rates continue upward trajectory)
- System cost and quality (higher quality = longer lifespan, better ROI)
- Self-consumption percentage (higher self-consumption = better ROI than export-focused systems)
- Maintenance costs (minimal for well-designed systems)
- Property value impact (solar often adds resale value, particularly in areas with high electricity costs)

**Example ROI Calculation**: A KES 400,000 grid-tie system offsetting 500 kWh/month at KES 20/kWh saves KES 10,000/month or KES 120,000/year. Payback: KES 400,000 ÷ KES 120,000 = ~3.3 years. Beyond year 3.3, system essentially provides free electricity for remaining warranty period (typically 20+ years).

## Installation and Implementation Process

### Site Assessment

Professional site assessment typically evaluates:

- **Roof condition and orientation**: Optimal: north-facing, angle ≈ 1°, no shading
- **Electrical infrastructure**: Existing distribution board, earthing adequacy, cable sizing
- **Consumption patterns**: Load assessment as discussed earlier
- **Shading analysis**: Tree growth, nearby structures, vent pipes affecting panel performance
- **Grid accessibility**: KPLC connection point, availability of net metering (where applicable)

**Many solar companies offer free or paid site assessments**; expect a thorough evaluation including roof measurements, shading analysis using specialist tools, and electrical system inspection.

### Permits and Approvals

**Required Approvals in Kenya**:

1. **EPRA registration**: Solar company must be EPRA-registered; consumer should verify
2. **County government approvals**: Some counties require permits for roof-mounted installations
3. **KPLC connection approval**: For grid-tie systems, approval to connect to distribution network
4. **Building approval** (if applicable): For new constructions or significant roof modifications

**Reputable solar companies** typically handle most permit processes as part of their service, but consumers should verify compliance.

### Installation Process

**Typical Timeline**: 3-10 days depending on system complexity and weather

**Key Steps**:
1. **Site preparation**: Roof safety measures, equipment staging
2. **Mounting structure installation**: Roof penetration (properly flashed to prevent leaks) or ground mounting setup
3. **Panel installation**: Mounting panels on structures, electrical connections in series/parallel as designed
4. **Inverter installation**: Near main electrical distribution, with proper ventilation and accessibility
5. **Battery installation** (if applicable): Ventilated, temperature-controlled location
6. **Electrical integration**: Connection to distribution board, installation of generation meter (if required), safety disconnects
7. **System commissioning**: Testing, performance verification, user education
8. **Documentation**: Providing as-built drawings, warranty documents, maintenance guidelines

**Typical Installation Team**: 2-3 technicians plus electrical engineer for grid-tie connections, completed over 2-5 days depending on complexity.

## Operation and Maintenance

### Daily Operation

**For Grid-Tie Systems**:
- Simple operation; system automatically generates power, supplies home loads, feeds excess to grid
- Monitor inverter display or app for production and consumption data
- No daily maintenance required

**For Off-Grid/Hybrid Systems**:
- Monitor battery state of charge, especially during extended cloudy periods
- Conscious load management during low-production periods
- Weekly visual inspection of panels (clear of debris, dust, bird droppings)

**Typical Daily Pattern**: Peak generation midday (10 AM - 4 PM), depending on season; evening usage when solar production diminishes; battery charging typically occurs midday.

### Cleaning and Maintenance

**Panel Cleaning**: Dust, bird droppings, and occasional leaf accumulation can reduce efficiency by 5-20% in Kenyan conditions.

**Recommended Cleaning Frequency**:
- **Dusty conditions** (dry seasons, harmattan): Every 2-3 months
- **Rainy season**: Natural cleaning may suffice, but inspect after heavy rains
- **Coastal areas**: Salt accumulation may require more frequent cleaning

**Cleaning Method**:
- Soft water or de-ionized water preferred (hard water spots)
- Soft brush or sponge, mild detergent if needed
- Ground-level cleaning using extended tools preferred over roof access
- Avoid abrasive materials or high-pressure washing that could damage panels

**Professional Cleaning Services**: Available from some solar companies, typically KES 2,000 - KES 5,000 per session for residential systems.

**Inverter Monitoring**: Most modern inverters provide monitoring via display or mobile app, alerting to performance issues, fault conditions, or maintenance needs.

**Battery Maintenance** (lead-acid only):
- **Water topping**: Every 3-6 months with distilled water (check electrolyte levels)
- **Equalization charging**: Monthly or as recommended by battery manufacturer
- **Terminal cleaning**: Regular terminal inspection and cleaning to prevent corrosion
- **Ventilation**: Ensure battery enclosure has adequate ventilation

**Battery Replacement**: Typically every 3-5 years for lead-acid, 8-15 years for lithium-ion. Plan for this major recurring cost in total cost of ownership calculations.

### Performance Monitoring

**Key Metrics to Track**:
- Daily energy production (kWh)
- Self-consumption percentage (what % of generated power is used vs. exported/exported)
- Grid import/export (for grid-tie systems)
- Performance ratio (actual vs. theoretical production, accounts for temperature, soiling, etc.)

**Monitoring Tools**:
- Inverter built-in display
- Manufacturer mobile apps (SolarEdge, Huawei, etc.)
- Third-party monitoring platforms
- Simple kWh meters between inverter and main distribution

**Alert Indicators**:
- Sudden production drop (>20% from expected)
- Unexpected grid imports (for grid-tie)
- Fault codes or warning lights on inverter
- Unusual battery behavior (for hybrid/off-grid)

## Kenyon Market-Specific Considerations

### Climate and Seasonal Variation

**Dry Seasons** (typically December-February, June-August):
- Highest solar production potential
- Dust accumulation on panels requires regular cleaning
- Lower humidity generally beneficial for panel efficiency

**Rainy Seasons** (typically March-May, October-November):
- Reduced daily production due to cloud cover
- Panel cleaning naturally assisted by rainfall
- Potential for water damage if installation not properly sealed

**Harmattan/Dry Wind Periods**: 
- Occur primarily in northern regions
- Significant dust deposition on panels
- More frequent cleaning may be necessary (monthly vs. quarterly)

**Temperature Effects**: 
- Panel efficiency decreases slightly with heat (-0.3% to -0.5%/°C above 25°C)
- Kenya's ambient temperatures (25-30°C average) have moderate effect
- Abundant sunlight more than compensates for efficiency losses
- Proper ventilation behind panels helps cool and maintain efficiency

### Power Grid Characteristics

**KPLC Supply Quality**:
- Urban areas: Generally reliable, occasional outages
- Rural areas: More frequent outages, voltage fluctuations
- Planned outages for maintenance are communicated in advance
- Unplanned outages occur, duration varies

**Solar Integration Considerations**:
- **Grid-tie systems** provide reduced grid dependence but not outage protection (unless hybrid)
- **Off-grid systems** provide complete independence but require greater investment
- **Hybrid systems** offer middle ground for outage protection without full off-grid cost
- **Voltage fluctuations** may affect sensitive electronics; consider surge protection

### Import Dynamics and Currency

**Solar Equipment in Kenya**:
- Significant portion imported (panels, inverters, batteries)
- **USD/KES exchange rate** significantly affects pricing (40-50% of system cost often USD-denominated)
- Local assemblers may offer some cost advantage by reducing import markup
- Price variations between suppliers (15-30% range common for same specifications)

**Strategic Timing**: Some buyers monitor exchange rates and time purchases during favorable rates, though waiting for optimal rates carries risk of further increases and delayed savings.

### Local Content and Supporting Kenyan Industry

**Growing Local Solar Industry**:
- Some local companies manufacture mounting structures, balance-of-system components
- Local assembly of battery systems using imported cells
- Growing pool of certified EPRA installers (increasing from few dozen to several hundred nationally)
- Local technical training programs developing solar workforce

**Benefits of Supporting Local**:
- Faster service response
- Better understanding of local conditions
- Contribution to Kenyan green economy
- Potential cost advantages in long term through local service networks

**Consideration**: Balance local support with component quality and cost; some high-tech components (inverters, panels) may have limited local manufacturing options.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How much roof space do I need for solar panels?

**Rule of thumb**: Approximately 10-12 m² of unshaded roof space per 1 kW of solar panels.

**Typical Kenyan roof sizes**:
- Standard 3-bedroom house roof: 40-60 m², sufficient for 3-6 kW system
- Smaller homes/terraces: May accommodate 1-3 kW system
- Ensure minimum 0.5m clearance from roof edges and obstacles

**Shading considerations**: Even partial shading on one panel can significantly reduce entire string output; micro-inverters or power optimizers can mitigate this at additional cost.

### Will solar work during power outages?

- **Grid-tie systems**: No, they automatically shut off for safety during grid outages (unless special hybrid inverter with backup capability)
- **Off-grid systems**: Yes, provides power continuously as long as sun shines and batteries have charge
- **Hybrid systems**: Yes, for selected circuits (typically essential loads like lighting, phone charging, refrigerator) depending on battery capacity and system design

### How long do solar panels last?

- **Performance warranty**: 25 years (manufacturer guarantees minimum output, typically 80-85% after 25 years)
- **Actual lifespan**: 30-35 years common, with gradual efficiency decline
- **Inverter lifespan**: 10-15 years (typically requires replacement once during system life)
- **Battery lifespan**: 3-5 years (lead-acid), 8-15 years (lithium-ion)

### Can I expand my solar system later?

Yes, most system types allow expansion, though considerations apply:

- **Grid-tie**: Easier to expand; inverter may need replacement if at capacity; additional panels added with matching specifications
- **Off-grid**: More complex; battery bank expansion possible but must match existing battery chemistry/voltage; may require new charge controller
- **Hybrid**: Expansion possible; battery addition feasible if hybrid inverter has capacity; panel addition straightforward if inverter has available input capacity

Always consult original installer for expansion feasibility and warranty implications.

### Do I need planning permission for solar panels in Kenya?

Requirements vary by county, but typically:

- **Roof-mounted systems**: Some counties require notification or minor permits; many now have streamlined processes
- **Ground-mounted**: More likely to require planning permission, especially for visible installations
- **Heritage/conservation areas**: Additional restrictions may apply
- **EPRA registration**: Required regardless of permit requirements

**Check with** your county government and engage a solar company familiar with local requirements.

### Can I sell excess solar power to KPLC?

Kenya has net metering/feed-in tariff policies, but specifics evolve:

- **Nairobi and surrounding areas**: Net metering increasingly available, terms vary
- **Other regions**: Feed-in tariffs or net metering availability expanding
- **Export rates**: Typically lower than retail purchase rates
- **Metering requirements**: Bi-directional meter required (provided/installation often included by solar company)

**Current status**: Check with KPLA and your solar provider for current net metering availability and rates in your area. Policies evolve, and staying informed is essential.

### Is solar viable in Kenya's coastal region?

Yes, coastal Kenya has good solar potential (4.5-5 kWh/m²/day), though considerations include:

- **Humidity**: Can affect panel soiling rate; more frequent cleaning may be needed
- **Salt air**: Can cause corrosion on mounting structures; choose marine-grade or properly coated components
- **Cyclone/storm risk**: Ensure mounting structure rated for local wind loads
- **Cooling effect**: Ocean breezes may slightly improve panel efficiency compared to inland areas

### What happens to my solar system if I move house?

- **Owned systems**: Transferable to new home (with possible re-assessment and minor modification) or can be sold as part of property
- **Financed/systems**: Contract transfer to new homeowner or buy-out options; discuss with financing institution
- **Rental situations**: Portable or temporary systems available; discuss with landlord for permanent installation

**Best practice**: Discuss solar with potential buyers/tenants; solar can be a selling point, particularly in areas with high electricity costs.

### Do solar panels increase property value?

Research suggests solar typically adds 3-5% to property value, particularly in:
- Areas with high electricity costs
- Properties with owned (not financed) solar systems
- Homes with good solar orientation and system condition
- Buyers prioritizing energy independence or sustainability

The impact varies by market and buyer priorities, but solar is increasingly viewed as a home improvement similar to renovated kitchen or bathroom.
